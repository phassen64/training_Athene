AI Concepts
Although AI concepts are technical in nature, you only need to grasp them from a high level-enough for you to be able to effectively communicate with your colleagues and clients.

What Is Artificial Intelligence (AI)?
Artificial intelligence (AI) is, at its core, the ability of machines to exhibit humanlike intelligence. What exactly is meant by "humanlike intelligence" is a source of much debate-some would even argue that true AI is not yet achievable, given strict requirements for "intelligence." And, the definition continues to evolve over time. However, there are some factors that make up a common understanding of "intelligence," including:
• The ability to perceive one's own environment.
• The ability to take actions for the purpose of achieving goals.
• The ability to obtain knowledge and apply it to a problem in order to solve that problem.
In a more practical sense, AI is a scientific discipline that governs the study, creation, and operation of machine intelligence. While this discipline has existed for decades, it has seen a recent resurgence in many different facets of society, including business. AI has the potential to revolutionize every sector of industry; in many cases, that revolution has already begun. Although AI is a highly technical field comprising many different complex elements, you don't need a great deal of technical knowledge to understand its impact and how you can leverage it in your organization.

AI-Enabling Technologies
As mentioned in the timeline, 2006 to 2008 saw the rise of three of the most important technologies for AI development and research: cloud computing, big data, and general-purpose GPUs. These deserve further explanation.
• Cloud computing is an approach to providing computing services and other resources over a network of distributed servers and other infrastructure. Businesses rely on cloud services for many, or even all, of their IT-related needs. Many organizations offload the computing costs of developing AI solutions to the cloud for two reasons: one, because AI, particularly deep learning, requires a lot of resources; and two, because the cloud can efficiently match the ever-changing demand for resources. Also, most organizations don't have the massive infrastructure that cloud providers like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft have.
• Graphics processing units (GPUs) are computer chips designed to process graphics, images, and other visual media. GPUs were originally created to support video game graphics, but most GPUs these days are considered general-purpose GPUs, meaning they can carry out tasks that were traditionally assigned to central processing units (CPUs). GPUs excel at AI processing tasks, whereas traditional CPUs tend to struggle. These tasks require a lot of memory bandwidth and a lot of linear algebra calculations-both things that GPUs do better than CPUs. Therefore, GPUs have become the de facto processing equipment for AI, particularly deep learning.

General AI and Narrow AI
There are many ways to categorize AI. One way is to define it in terms of its scope and overall ability. When doing so, AI is typically categorized as either being general or narrow.
Artificial general intelligence (AGI), also called general AI or strong AI, refers to machines that have a similar level of cognitive abilities as humans. This means the machine has the ability to use reason-not just to solve one or a few problems, but to extrapolate those reasoning abilities to solve many novel problems. A machine with AGI would be able to fool a human observer into thinking that the machine itself is human. At the moment, AGI is far from being a reality. It is debatable as to whether or not it will ever be a reality. Nevertheless, much philosophical thought and scientific research is being done on the subject, including whether or not a general AI system would truly possess consciousness or sapience.
Narrow AI, also called weak AI, is AI that is able to solve one or a few specific problems that are assigned to it by humans. It can approximate certain human traits, including reason, but it does not truly possess those traits. Instead, a weak AI system is limited in scope and cannot be applied to a general set of tasks like AGI. Still, even systems with limited scope can be very powerful. This is where AI is at now and will likely be for the foreseeable future. So, when people talk about implementing AI in business, they are referring to narrow AI, not the thinking machines of science fiction.
Example
Consider the example of an AI-powered machine cleaning a house. There are autonomous vacuum cleaners on the market today that can navigate a room by themselves, avoiding obstacles and detecting dirty spots on the floor that need cleaning. These AI systems excel at determining how best to go about cleaning a floor. This is an example of narrow AI because the vacuum robot is good at the one thing it was designed to do. It cannot do anything else, at least not very well.
Now consider a robot that has never vacuumed before. It is able to enter a house (after having been given a key), find the vacuum cleaner in the closet, clean out the dust container if it's full, replace the filter if the vacuum has one and it's old, begin cleaning the floors room by room, empty out the dust that accumulated, put the vacuum back in the closet, then leave the house and lock the door. That robot would possess AGI because it would be able to perform multiple distinct tasks, similar to how an adult human would vacuum a house if they had never vacuumed it before.

Machine Learning
Machine learning is an AI discipline that makes predictions and other decisions about an environment based on a given set of data. These predictions are made without the aid of instructions programmed directly into the machine itself. Instead, the machine is able to process input data using one or more algorithms-sets of rules for carrying out problem-solving operations
-that can make intelligent decisions about that data and how it pertains to an environment.

You can also think of machine learning in terms of how it compares to traditional rule-based programming. In the latter, a programmer might create a series of if-then statements for decision making: If this happens, then do that. With machine learning, a programmer doesn't write these rules directly. The model makes a decision automatically based on what it has learned.

Machine Learning Datasets
As mentioned, machine learning models are created by putting input data through an algorithm. But what exactly is being "learned" by the machine? Consider the following example: You have a spreadsheet of health data for individuals who participated in a study. The data is in a table, with each person as a row and various characteristics of each person as columns (e.g., weight, height, and blood-pressure level). There are two dimensions to this data:
• Example-the individual instance of data. This corresponds to rows in a table, and in the health-records scenario, refers to individual people.
• Feature-each measurable property or characteristic of an example. This corresponds to columns in a table, and in the health-records scenario, refers to a person's body weight, height, blood-pressure level, etc.
Put together, this dataset is fed into the algorithm as input. The resulting model identifies the patterns in the features for each example and uses these patterns to determine how to make predictions on new data. For example, the model might learn how a person's body weight relates to their blood-pressure level-not just for a specific person, but in general. That way, the model can make informed predictions about a new patient based on their body weight and blood-pressure level.
The process by which a model learns from input data is called training.

Deep Learning
Deep learning is a type of machine learning that makes decisions using multiple layers of information. These layers are arranged as a hierarchy of decisions or other tasks, where the conclusions drawn from a lower layer can influence the conclusions drawn at higher layers. This makes a deep learning model more complex than a traditional machine learning model.
Consider the example of a model that processes an image of a human face. It will organize aspects of a face into different layers, starting with simple shapes like lines and curves and moving on from there. Eventually, at the highest layers, it will start identifying more recognizable features: eyes, lips, ears, a nose, etc. The deep learning model eventually is able to determine whether or not an image contains a human face based on what it has learned.
Deep learning moves past tabular datasets like the health records shown earlier. Instead, deep learning datasets usually consist of unstructured data-images, video, audio, text, and other data that can't fit neatly into a spreadsheet.

Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)
Deep learning is accomplished through the use of artificial neural networks. An artificial neural network (ANN) is a machine approximation of biological neural networks-like the connective structure of the human brain-for the purpose of learning. Like the brain, ANNs include nodes that can transmit information to other nodes, called neurons. Artificial neurons are connected to one another similar to how biological neurons are connected via synapses. These neurons are grouped into the various layers mentioned earlier. Each layer can perform a specific function and share its output with other layers to create a highly integrated platform for making intelligent decisions.
Although each individual neuron may be relatively simple, a network comprising millions upon millions of interconnected neurons is able to perform very complex calculations that are not always possible with standard machine learning models.
In the context of deep learning, the ANN is the model. When new input data is sent to the ANN, it travels through each layer, where the neurons perform calculations on the input. At the end of the network, the output layer provides the result the model was built for. In the prior example, this means classifying an input image as including a face or not including a face.

Parameters and Weights
Machine learning models, including ANNs, are often described in terms of the number of parameters they are built with. A model's parameters are the values that it learns during training, which are then used to transform input data and output a result. Oftentimes, the more parameters a model has, the better it will be at its assigned task. This goes back to the idea of big data and how training a model on as much data as possible usually (but not always) leads to better results.
All machine learning models have parameters. In neural networks, the parameters are the connections between neurons in the network. The most important parameters in neural networks are called weights. Weights are numbers and can be thought of as representing the "strength" of the connection between each neuron. The greater the magnitude of number (positive or negative), the stronger the connection. The weights are what transform the data as it passes through each layer, until it gets to the output.
Weights are not the only parameter in a neural network, though the two words are often used interchangeably. When someone says that a new AI model was trained on 100 billion parameters, they are describing the overall size of the network. Knowing the number of parameters in a model can help you determine the effectiveness of that model compared to others. This is not always the case, but a decent rule of thumb is that the more parameters a model has, the better it is at its assigned task. Also, if someone says that a model's weights are freely available, they are essentially saying that anyone can reconstruct the network and directly use it themselves, rather than rely on a third-party service.

Hyperparameters
You might also encounter the term hyperparameter. A hyperparameter is a configuration set on a machine learning algorithm before training. They are used by machine learning engineers to tune the resulting model so that it is more effective at its assigned task. One example of a hyperparameter is the number of layers in a neural network. Be careful not to confuse model parameters/weights with hyperparameters.

Model Checkpoints
When machine learning engineers build models, they need a way to share those models with other systems, applications, and users. How they save a model will depend on the programming library or other tool they used to construct it, but generally, they will have the option to save these models as checkpoints. A model checkpoint is the state of the model's parameters saved to a file.
Software developers can incorporate model checkpoints into their own apps. This enables the app to load the model in a customized environment, then open the model up to accepting inputs from and providing outputs to users as needed. In other words, checkpoints are a way for organizations to leverage the power of a model directly, whether that model is created by internal personnel or an external third party.
The size of a checkpoint file is related to the size of the model (i.e., the number of parameters). Some checkpoints, particularly for large neural networks, can be several gigabytes (GB) in storage size.


Note: There are other ways to save machine learning models, but checkpoints tend to be the most common for distribution.

The most notable difference between traditional machine learning and deep learning has to do with features. Generative AI Concepts
Now that you've learned the fundamentals of AI in a general sense, you can begin exploring the concepts behind its fastest-growing and most talked-about mode: generative AI.

Generative AI
One remarkably popular application of deep learning in recent years is generative AI. Generative AI refers to deep learning models (i.e., artificial neural networks) that are designed to generate any kind of content-text, programming code, audio, images, video, and so on. In many cases, the goal is for this generated content to appear as if it were created by a human.
Most generative models are trained on massive amounts of data that correspond to whatever the model is designed to generate. A model like GPT-4o that generates text is trained on textual sources like scientific papers, textbooks, novels, social media posts, chat logs, and potentially much more. A model like DALL·E that generates images is trained on photographs, hand drawings, computer-generated images (CGI), and more. Either one could use NLP, computer vision, or a mix of the two.
The power of generative AI is that any user, regardless of technical ability, can "ask" the model to create whatever it is the user wants. There are different ways of "asking" a model to do something, including:
• Providing a written explanation in the form of a prompt to the model.
• Providing a piece of media (like an image) to use as a baseline or example for the model to start from.
• Providing a piece of media (like an audio recording) that the model can transform into some other media (like a transcript).
• Using some other tool (like a word processor) and receiving suggestions from the model based on your work.
• Any combination of these, and potentially more.
At first it might seem like generative AI is more of a toy or novelty, but it actually holds a great deal of potential in business applications. Being able to quickly generate content in many different forms can increase efficiency, save time, and unlock new opportunities for an organization.

Generative AI Prompting
Perhaps the most common method for extracting generated content out of a generative AI model is prompting. Prompting is the process of providing text as input to the model, which influences what the model produces on output. Prompts are usually textual descriptions of the thing you'd like to generate. They can range from being very short (a few words) to much longer, depending on the model's capabilities. The model translates the words and phrases you've provided in the prompt into some type of content that (ideally) embodies those words and phrases.
For example, you might prompt the model to write a science-fiction short story about explorers in space who discover an alien civilization, or you might prompt a different model to create a realistic photograph of a monkey with a small hat typing at a typewriter in an office. You're only limited by how effective the model is at understanding your prompt and translating it into useful content.
Generative models have a certain degree of randomness to them. The overall "theme" of the images stayed true to the prompt, however. Depending on the model you're using, you may be given the option to make the output deterministic-in other words, the same exact prompt will always lead to the same exact output.
However, for the purposes of simulating human creativity, some variation is often desirable.

Prompt Engineering
Generative models don't provide perfect results every time, and there is a sort of art to writing effective prompts. This art is known as prompt engineering. The ability to effectively write a prompt that produces high-quality and desirable output from a model is an important skill for anyone looking to use generative AI, not just technical personnel.

What constitutes a "good" prompt is not easy to pin down, nor is it universal. It's very difficult, even impossible, for a human-including AI experts-to fully understand why a neural network makes the decisions it does. So, figuring out what exact words to type into a prompt to get the exact output you're looking for is likewise not easy.
Nevertheless, there are some best practices you can use to guide your prompting. There are also prompting techniques that apply to different types of generative AI, as you'll see later.

Build prompts iteratively.	Good prompting is an iterative process. You cannot
expect to create the perfect prompt on your very first try. In some cases, you'll need to refine your prompt over multiple attempts based on the outputs you receive. If the output to your first prompt looks good but is missing a key element, you might rewrite your prompt to emphasize that missing element. If the output is still not satisfactory, you might change other aspects of the prompt until you get what you're looking for out of the model.
Be descriptive.	It's possible for a model to generate what you want by providing it with just a few words in the prompt, but this is not a reliable approach. Instead, you should try leading the model through the problem you want it to solve by being descriptive. Even small details can influence the model's outputs in a direction that is closer to what you're looking for.
Prefer clarity to verbosity.	As important as being descriptive is, you don't need to
go overboard. Being clear in what you're asking is almost always more important than the length of the prompt. Use precise and direct language to explain what it is you want or do not want.
Provide relevant examples.	If you're not getting good results from the model, or
those results are not in the desired form, try providing examples in the prompt whenever possible. This is especially helpful when what you're asking for is fairly complex. If you can include or at least reference any relevant examples, the model will have a framework to draw from.
Save and reuse successful prompts.	If you've found success in applying one or more
prompts to solve a problem, you can reuse those prompts to solve a similar problem. That way, you can quickly and easily get the desired outputs without having to start from scratch.

In-Context Prompting
In-context prompting is a technique in which one or more examples are provided to a model to guide it toward a desired output. The examples are provided as pairs ("shots") that consist of a problem and a solution. This technique is used with models that generate text output from text input.
The different methods of in-context prompting are as follows:
• Zero-shot prompting: No examples are provided, i.e., the model is not guided.
<Text of customer complaint.> Why is the customer asking for a refund?
• One-shot prompting: One example is provided.
Complaint: The product was broken when it arrived. Reason: Defect. Complaint: <text of customer complaint.> Reason:
• Few-shot prompting: Multiple examples are provided.
Complaint: The product was broken when it arrived. Reason: Defect. Complaint: I ordered the red version but got the blue one instead. Reason: Wrong color.
Complaint: <text of customer complaint.> Reason:
Notice how, for one-shot and few-shot prompting, the model is asked to finish the Reason: prompt based on what it has inferred from the provided example(s). In this case, the purpose is to get the model to format the reason in a concise way, whereas in the zero-shot example, it might respond with too much verbiage. An automated system might be set up to collect complaints from a database and send them to the generative AI model along with in-context examples. It can then retrieve the complaint reasons in a way that is better formatted for database storage.


Note: You may encounter the word "learning" used in place of "prompting" when people refer to this concept (e.g., "in-context learning" and "zero-shot learning"). This is somewhat misleading, as "learning" implies that the model is undergoing training, i.e., that its parameters are being updated. In this case, no parameters are being updated; the example provided in the prompt is merely guiding the model's output.
Fine Tuning
One of generative AI's strengths is its support for fine tuning. A model that is fine tuned has had its weights updated by learning from a new dataset.
There are several benefits to this. Fine tuning can augment an existing model with more training examples to increase the usefulness of its outputs. The model can also be customized with specialized data so that its outputs more closely reflect that specialized need. For example, most of the major foundational models available from companies like OpenAI and Google are trained on generalized data. An individual or organization could fine tune these models with data that is specific to their own domain in order to make the output more useful to them. A side benefit of this is that it reduces or outright eliminates the need to perform in-context prompting, since the model is actually learning the desired context when it undergoes fine tuning. This also leads to faster response times from the model since it doesn't need to infer the proper context for each prompt.
The ability to fine tune a model depends on the model you're starting with. Some models are not available for fine tuning, so you'll need to check with the model's provider.
It's important to understand that fine tuning requires a lot of time and preparatory work. The new training data needs to be prepared so that it's in a format suitable for the model. And, it can take a lot of time and resources to actually perform the fine tuning. Still, if you have the data, time, and capability, you should at least consider creating a fine-tuned model.

Generative AI Approaches
Generative AI models can be built using different algorithms, techniques, and frameworks. There are three major approaches to generative AI that have found success in recent years. Although you don't need to dive into the complexities behind each approach, it helps to know what those approaches are from a high level.
• Diffusion models
• Generative pre-trained transformers (GPTs)
• Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)


Note: These are not the only approaches used in generative AI, but they are the most common.
Diffusion Models
Diffusion models are the latest in image-generating, neural-network architectures. They are primarily based on the concepts of noising and denoising through two separate processes.
The first step involves the forward diffusion process, in which the neural network incrementally applies random noise to an input image during training. Eventually, the image looks nearly indecipherable to a human observer. Then, the fully noised image is sent through a reverse denoising process that incrementally reconstructs the image until it returns to its original state. After training, new random noise can be sent through the reverse denoising process in order to generate new images.
Diffusion models tend to be slower at generating outputs compared to older architectures, but they also produce higher-quality images that can generalize very well to many domains. This makes them excellent to use as foundational models that can be fine tuned for more specialized applications.
Many of the popular image-generating tools that emerged in 2022 are based on a diffusion architecture.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)
A generative adversarial network (GAN) is an older neural network architecture that pits two different neural networks against each other. One network is a discriminative model (the discriminator), and the other is a generative model (the generator). The generator's intent is to "fool" the discriminator into thinking the new data is authentic data from the actual training set. The discriminator, meanwhile, attempts to spot any "forgeries" that the generator gives it. The act of training these networks in an oppositional way leads to highly effective models whose skill surpasses those of standard neural networks.
GANs are primarily applied to computer-vision problems, but can also be applied to audio processing and NLP. In fact, they were one of the first types of neural networks that were successful at generating lifelike images. However, their popularity has been on the decline in favor of other approaches like diffusion, which tend to be even more successful at image generation.
Variational Autoencoders (VAE)
Another type of model used primarily for image generation are variational autoencoders (VAEs). Like GANs, VAEs consist of two neural networks. Unlike GANs, they are not pitted against each other. The first network is called the encoder, and the second is called the decoder. They work together to learn what are called latent representations, or the simplified versions of images in the network. During training, the encoder network converts an input image into its latent representation, and the decoder uses this to try to reconstruct the original input. After training, latent representations can then be sent to the decoder, enabling it to generate new images.
Compared to GANs, VAEs may not produce the same quality of results and may be slower at producing outputs. However, VAEs are better at generalizing. This means they can produce more varied outputs across a variety of domains, whereas a GAN is best suited to a specific domain. A GAN might be very good at generating pictures of dogs, but a VAE will likely be better at generating pictures of any animal, or even other kinds of subjects. VAEs are not as popular as diffusion models, though they can be used to enhance diffusion models.

Generative Pre-trained Transformers (GPT)
Generative pre-trained transformers (GPTs) are neural networks that excel at processing and generating text. They form the backbone of many prominent models that use text-based and code-based modalities, including OpenAI's GPT-n models. GPTs are a version of a transformer architecture. A transformer architecture is designed to process sequential input, such as language. A sentence is sequential data because each successive word builds upon the prior words in a sentence. However, transformers differ from older language processors in that they process the sequence all at once instead of piece by piece, focusing on the context of each position in the sequence. This technique is called self-attention.
The "pre-trained" part of a GPT means that the network was initially trained on very large datasets of unlabeled text, and that the network can later be fine tuned on smaller labeled datasets for specialized purposes.
To generate new text, a GPT takes an input sequence of text and then determines what the next word is. After that word is generated, it can generate the next word, and so on. It does this by encoding the input into a form that the network can more effectively process, applying the transformer architecture to obtain predictions for the next word, and then decoding that word into a form that is understandable to the user.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is not a type of generative network architecture, but a technique used by some neural networks to enhance their output capabilities, particularly in text-based language models. As the name suggests, neural networks use human feedback to assess the quality of the model's outputs rather than relying on just learning from training data. RLHF is similar to the traditional reinforcement learning mentioned earlier in that it uses rewards to guide a model toward a desirable set of actions and/or discourage it from an undesirable set of actions.
Training a reward model requires human evaluators to apply a rank or score to several responses to an input prompt. The reward model then tries to predict the scores for each of the language model's outputs. The reward model eventually gets better at predicting these scores, which it can then use to help the main language model produce better outputs by selecting the response that it predicts will be most useful.
In the context of GPTs, you can think of RLHF as the last of three "steps" toward improving the effectiveness of a model, where each successive step makes improvements to the prior step.
1. Pre-train a model with very large unlabeled datasets.
2. Fine tune a model with a smaller set of labeled datasets.
3. Refine the model using human feedback.
Not all steps are necessary, but combining RLHF with the other two steps almost always leads to optimal performance. However, designing an effective reward system for the model can be challenging, as what constitutes a "good" prompt can change from person to person.

Agentic AI
Agentic AI is the latest emerging approach to artificial intelligence in the business world. It refers to AI systems that can, with limited human oversight, make decisions based on input and act on those decisions autonomously in the pursuit of one or more goals. The agents in an agentic system are the subsystems that perform the automated tasks. Agents are context aware, meaning they can understand a specific environment-like an application-and act based on changing conditions in that environment. Unlike traditional agents, which are based on hardcoded programming rules, AI-driven agents can adapt and improve over time, making future actions better.
Agentic AI is considered a distinct discipline, although it shares much in common with generative AI. In fact, the technology used in generative systems, like large language models, also underlies agentic systems. You can think of agentic AI as going a step beyond generative AI-whereas generative AI reacts to human-provided prompts and outputs content, agentic AI actively makes multi-step changes to an environment based on various sources of input.
There are numerous benefits to agentic AI, including its ability to increase the speed and scale of tasks; minimize disruptions by adapting to changes; bridge gaps between data and tools; and free humans up to focus on creative work instead of tedious, routine tasks.

There are several ways an organization can avail itself of generative or agentic AI. Identify Text Generation Concepts
There are multiple aspects of text generation that you should become familiar with. This will help you select the aspects that will be most appropriate for your use of generative AI. And, you'll get a chance to use one or more of the most prominent tools for generating text using AI.

Large Language Models (LLMs)
Large language models (LLMs) are deep learning models trained on very large bodies of text in order to determine how best to predict some kind of textual output by analyzing patterns in input data. This makes them suitable for answering questions, solving problems, producing creative works, and much more.
LLMs are usually the product of a generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) architecture. Their ability to generalize depends on what data they were trained on, but most LLMs are trained on a variety of textual sources (Internet posts, published books, science articles, etc.) so that they can function as foundational models.
LLMs are at the forefront of generative AI. They have found great success in recent years, and AI researchers have gradually improved their ability to generate humanlike text. These improvements are accomplished by training the model on more volumes of text as well as making improvements to the GPT architecture itself. The largest LLMs have trillions of parameters and require immense resources to train.
Mixture of Experts (MoE)
You may come across the term mixture of experts (MoE) when used to describe generative AI models, particularly LLMs. MoE refers to a design approach to neural networks that incorporates multiple subnetworks ("experts") that each specialize in different tasks or inputs. Only a few experts are used at a time, which makes it possible for very large models to run faster and more efficiently.

Tokens
LLMs and other models that use natural-language processing (NLP) don't just process individual words or sentences. They process tokens, which are small sequences of written language that have been partitioned from the whole. The process of converting text into tokens is called tokenization.
Consider the following sentence:
Artificial neural networks power generative AI.
If you were to partition this sentence naturally, you might say there's six total words. When this sentence is tokenized, you might get something like the following figure.

Artificial neural networks power generative AI. 

Figure 2-1: Nine tokens extracted from the sample sentence. The colors indicate the boundaries of each token.
Now, instead of six words, there are nine tokens. The larger words, like "artificial" and "generative," are broken up into two tokens. The smaller words, like "neural" and "power," correspond to a single token. The period at the end of the sentence is also considered its own token. The LLM will use these tokens as part of the process of encoding text. This makes it easier for the LLM to analyze the text and predict the next token as output.

Additional Text-Generation Approaches
Aside from those just mentioned, there are several more ways to prompt a text-generation model to achieve a certain outcome.
• Summarization: Ask the model to summarize text you provide as input, like an email message or news article, so that you can get the gist of that text without having to read the entire thing.
• Inference: Ask the model to derive conclusions from data or premises, like providing it the medical report for a drug trial and asking it to identify instances of unexpected side effects.
• Evaluation: Ask the model to provide an "opinion" on some text or other content, such as whether or not you should make changes to a résumé you provide.
• Translation: Ask the model to translate text from one natural language to another, like translating a Spanish sentence into English.
• Troubleshooting: Ask the model to diagnose technical and other issues, like your computer not starting and giving you an error message.
• Sentiment analysis: Ask the model to determine the feelings people have about something, like whether their written review of a movie is positive or negative.
• Content generation: Ask the model to generate content that is meant to be directly consumed by an audience, such as a story, a speech, an email, a press release, a slogan, etc.

Best Practices for Prompt Engineering with Text Generation
Some best practices for prompt engineering with text-generation models include:
• Assign a chatbot a persona or role: As explained earlier, chatbots can adopt personas or roles. By asking a chatbot to pretend to be someone or something, it might return results that are more in line with what you're looking for.
• Write as clearly as you can: Most models can cope with the occasional spelling or grammar mistake, but just like with a person, too many errors can lead to confusion. Consider reviewing your prompts before you submit them.
• Provide any relevant conditions: In some cases, a model might follow your request too rigidly and output a response that doesn't have the nuance you're looking for. You may need to give the model certain conditions related to the problem, like telling it to summarize a news article using four bullet points.
• Ask for sources: If you're asking a model to research factual information, it doesn't hurt to ask it for sources. If it provides those sources, you can use them to verify the accuracy of its claims.
• Know the model's restrictions: Most commercial LLMs have restrictions in place to prevent profane, offensive, and/or abusive material from being discussed. There may be other restrictions in place as well, including the fact that the model may not have trained on recent data, so you should prepare to adjust your prompts accordingly.
• Keep trying until you get it right: Try not to be discouraged if the model doesn't give you exactly the response you're looking for up front. Remember, prompt engineering is an iterative process. The more you work on your prompts, the better they'll be.

Reasoning Models
Reasoning models, also called reasoning language models (RLMs), or large reasoning models (LRMs), are a newer type of LLM designed to break down complex problems step by step before providing a response. Reasoning models follow a chain of thought that emulates human reasoning capabilities. They decompose a complex task into smaller subtasks, exploring and evaluating possible approaches to completing the subtasks. They may also backtrack to change their assumptions about a prior subtask based on new information in other subtasks. Some reasoning models can also re-evaluate their own initial output and refine it. Eventually, they arrive at an ultimate solution to the problem.
In the earliest days of LLMs, chain of thought was one of the most powerful prompt-engineering techniques for solving complex problems because it encouraged the model to think through the problem, which would often lead to better answers. With reasoning models, this is no longer necessary, as the chain of thought is built in. Reasoning models tend to be slower, as they need more time to "think," but they usually produce more accurate and useful results where sophisticated logic is required. The kinds of problems they are better equipped to solve include financial risk modeling, strategic planning, complex diagnostics, and more.
The first reasoning model was OpenAI's o1, but reasoning models from the other major AI players soon followed. Such models are specifically trained or fine tuned on logical, multi-step reasoning processes, like solving math puzzles, debugging code, and so on.


Note: Depending on the tool, you may need to explicitly select the reasoning model if you want to use it-e.g., as of writing, the "Think for longer" tool in ChatGPT unlocks this ability.
Note: Reasoning models are not necessarily superior to their non-reasoning counterparts. Some research has shown that letting a model "think" for longer can actually degrade output quality.

• Perform a specific task or tasks on a specific set of knowledge.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a technique that enhances traditional LLMs by retrieving relevant, up-to-date information from a knowledge base before generating a response. Instead of relying solely on the data it was trained with, the model can use a live repository of documentation, database entries, and more, to improve its accuracy and usefulness. RAG also helps minimize the need for fine tuning on new data, as the model can just reference any specialized data it needs, on the fly, to solve a problem. Likewise, there's no need to include the entire body of knowledge within the prompt itself.
If you've used one of the major chatbots recently, you've probably noticed the ability for it to search the web. This is RAG in action-the model can crawl various websites (or search-result metadata from websites), review the text, then incorporate that knowledge into its response. So, you can get up-to-date information about specific events and facts that is potentially more accurate than if the model was only exposed to its training data. RAG is also very useful in situations where a business needs to quickly reference processes, procedures, and other specifics that are currently buried under a mountain of documentation. Identify Image and Video Generation Concepts
To start with, you'll explore the different techniques associated with generating images and video, as well as the tools you can use to apply these techniques.

Text to Image
One of the most common approaches to generating images using AI is to provide a text-based prompt that describes the image you want the AI to create. The description normally includes at least a subject (like a person, place, or thing), but may also include:
• The medium (photograph, drawing, watercolor painting, etc.).
• The style (romanticized, modern, postmodern, etc.).
• The color profile (vivid, black and white, grayscale, etc.).
• The artist/photographer (van Gogh, Picasso, Ansel Adams, etc.).
• Additional aesthetic properties (moody, hazy, bright, etc.).
• Additional details (chairs, buildings, crowds, etc.).
• Positioning (in front of, off to the side, next to, etc.).
• Quality markers (high resolution, high detail, sharp focus, etc.).

Image Inpainting
In some cases, a model might output a perfectly adequate image that you'll be able to use. Other times, the image is mostly fine, but isn't quite acceptable. It would be nice to be able to edit the image to change or "fix" the parts you don't like. Thankfully, some image-generation models enable you to do just that. Instead of spending time getting the image to look just right using a traditional image editor, you can do everything with AI.
One of the most common techniques for editing images using AI is called inpainting. Inpainting involves painting over an area of the image you'd like to change, then instructing the model to change that painted area-also called a mask-in some way. The model then attempts to leave the unmasked part of the image alone and focus on changing the masked part so that the "good" aspects of the image remain intact. You can also do the inverse: tell the model to inpaint every part of the image except the mask.
Recall the bowl of oranges that had a stray lime in it. Every other part of the image is fine, but let's say you really want that lime to be an orange. The following figure shows a mask that was drawn over the top of the lime, which appears as a black region in the image. Drawing the mask is the same as using a brush in any given paint program. When you inpaint, you would typically provide a text prompt that tells the model what to do with the masked area. In this case, it just needs to create an orange, so that's the only word used in the inpainting prompt. The result is that the lime turned into an orange, whereas everything else in the image stayed the same.

Image Upscaling
Image upscaling is the process of increasing the resolution of an image while maintaining its aspect ratio. An image's resolution is the total number of pixels in that image, usually expressed as the number of pixels in width by the number of pixels in height. For example, a 1920 × 1280 image is 1,920 pixels wide and 1,280 pixels tall. Aspect ratio is the measurement of one dimension relative to the other dimension, usually expressed in a reduced form. So, a 1920 × 1280 image has an aspect ratio of 3:2, but can also be depicted as the fraction 3/2, which is 1.5. For every 3 pixels in width, there are 2 pixels in height. The image is therefore wider than it is tall.
Image upscaling is an attempt at improving the quality and/or fidelity of an image. The higher the resolution, the more detail there is in the image, which usually translates to an image that looks subjectively "better." Upscaling is commonly used to take a low-resolution image and make it larger. This is of particular importance in generative AI, because most models output images that are constrained to lower resolutions. For example, a model might be good at outputting images that are around 1,024 pixels in either dimension, with a little room for variation to account for images that don't have a square (1:1) aspect ratio, like 1024 × 1536 or 1408 × 1024. Models are usually trained on low-resolution images to reduce training time and hardware requirements.
AI models that perform upscaling existed before image-generation models. These AI upscaling models try to fill in detail by learning the patterns of neighboring pixels and using their knowledge of the training data to give a "best guess." They can be fairly effective, but the lower the starting resolution, the more difficult it is for the model to generate convincing levels of pixel detail. An alternative to this approach is to combine these upscaling models with generative approaches like image control. Certain image-control techniques can essentially reconstruct a low-resolution source image using a generative model, while also sending the reconstructed data to an upscaler. The results

Videos are composed of still images, usually referred to as frames. Many of the image-generation techniques just discussed are therefore applicable to generating video. However, the process for actually creating a series of frames that show movement often involves extra steps. And, keep in mind that video generation is one of the most difficult modalities for generative AI to work with, so the results tend to be less convincing than still images.
The following is a brief overview of the types of techniques that are involved in video generation, and which are likely to be more effective as generative technologies advance.

• Text to video: In this approach, you provide a text prompt much like you would using an image generator. The model constructs frames based on this prompt and then assembles them into a video. The result is a series of generated images that simulate motion.
• Script to video: This is different than a text-to-video approach; you're still providing text as input, but that text is more like a script that contains dialogue. The script doesn't tell the model what to depict; it tells the model how to depict an existing concept, usually a simulated person speaking the script to the viewer.
• Image to video: This approach involves presenting a model with a single image and having the model animate that image. The model applies inference on the image to determine how to depict it in motion across multiple frames. For example, supplying an image of a car on a road may cause the model to generate successive frames in which the car appears to be moving forward on the road.
• Text + image to video: By providing a text prompt alongside an image, you can guide the model toward creating the desired video. For example, you might specify in the text prompt that the car should be moving backward.
• Video to video: You might use this approach to transform a video into another video that looks similar, much like standard image-to-image generation.
• Text + video to video: Adding a text prompt alongside a reference video can help you specify the desired outcome of the video generation, much like a text + image-to-video approach. The difference is that this approach will already have motion data as part of the input, which can lead to results that more closely match the reference video.
• Frame interpolation: Frame interpolation is the process of filling in the "gaps" between two frames by generating one or more intermediate frames between them. This has two main applications for generative AI. The first is to increase the frame rate of existing video to make its motion look smoother. The other involves taking two still images as input and then having an AI attempt to interpolate frames between each image to simulate motion. For example, if you had one image of a car close to the camera (the starting point), and one image of the same car farther away (the finish), you could use interpolation to generate a video of that car moving from start to finish.
• Video customization: This is an umbrella term that refers to various techniques involved in modifying videos rather than generating new ones from scratch. For example, you can inpaint videos much like you can with images to change specific aspects. You can also remove or change backgrounds, change styles, apply texture and detail to raw animations, and so on.

Negative Prompting
Negative prompting is essentially the opposite of how prompts thus far have been shown-instead of instructing the model what to create, it instructs the model what not to create. This enables you to constrain the image output more because the negative prompt helps to remove or reduce the presence of unwanted elements. Some models are more sensitive to negative prompting than others.
You can fill out a negative prompt in the same way as a positive prompt. You supply the words and terms you want the model to avoid-everything from subjects, to details, to artistic styles, and so on. The negative and positive prompts are sent to the model together, so the combination of both is what helps to narrow the output down. Alternatively, if you're supplying a single prompt, like when using a chatbot, you can tell the model in plain language what not to include.

Image Interrogation
Writing effective prompts for image and video generation is a difficult task, perhaps more so than text and code generation. It's not always clear how to describe something visual in words, or even what to include or omit in that description. You also need to take into account what labels the model you're using was trained on, as those will form the concepts the model understands when prompted. This is where image interrogation comes in.
Image interrogation is the process by which a model analyzes an image and outputs text that describes the image. It is essentially the reverse of text to image-in other words, image to text. In the past, humans would need to do this manually for proper labeling, but interrogation models have become so effective that this process can be almost completely automated.

You're not sure how to write a prompt that would lead you to this image, however. Best Practices for Prompt Engineering with Image and Video Generation
There are some best practices you should consider when generating images and video from prompts.
• Be as descriptive as possible: The old saying "a picture is worth a thousand words" is surprisingly applicable to the world of image and video generation. Providing clear and descriptive details in your prompts will almost always lead to better results. If you want an object to be included in an output image, mention the object in the prompt. If you want the image to have a certain style, say so in the prompt.
• Research and reuse effective prompts: It's worth looking into the work that other people have done before you. There may be "universal prompts" or prompt templates out there that are known to be very effective at producing lifelike, realistic, or otherwise desirable images.
• Ask an LLM to write prompts: Just like many other tasks that involve text, an LLM can help you write a good text-to-image prompt. This is especially true if you're not sure how to describe various aspects in the image you're looking for. For example, if you saw a Picasso painting and wanted something in a cubist style, but didn't know who the artist was or the name of the style, you could describe the desired image as being "made of cubes." The LLM will probably understand you're looking for a cubist painting in the style of Picasso, which could then be added to the text-to-image prompt.
• Use reference images whenever possible: Reference images used in a text + image-to-image process are sort of like in-context prompting for LLMs; they give the model a baseline to start from. If you can, find images that have the desired composition, theme, style, or subject. This will save you effort in trying to write the perfect text prompt.
• Consider image aspect ratio: In some models, you have a choice of aspect ratio to generate on output. Even if you don't, you should find out what the default aspect ratio is. This is because

Fine Tuning Image and Video Models
There are several reasons why you might want to fine tune an image- or video-generation model. If your organization has its own library of images, such as stock photos that depict people, places, and objects that are relevant to what the organization produces, you could fine tune a foundational model so that it is more likely to generate images with these subjects. Or, perhaps you have a library of images that adopt a certain style, and you want to be able to easily replicate that style when generating images.
Another approach to fine tuning is to create a model that depicts a single subject that you want to reuse when generating images with a foundational model. Depending on the data used to train a foundational model, it may already recognize very famous people, so adding their names to the prompt could produce worthwhile results. But, more likely, you'll want to use a subject that the foundational model wasn't trained on. For example, you might want to depict the same person in a series of images so that those images are consistent. You could fine tune a model that is able to learn that person's appearance, then you'd give that person a name (or any other label). You could then use this label in a prompt to generate images with that specific person in it.
Whatever the approach you use, you should keep in mind that fine tuning a model requires labeled image data. This means that the images you use in training must be properly described using words and terms. You could do this manually, but keep in mind that image interrogation essentially does this automatically. Also, you may need to have a large number of images available for training for the fine-tuned model to be effective.

TOPIC A
Identify Audio Generation Concepts
Like other forms of generative AI, there are multiple techniques that make up the process of generating audio. In this lesson, you'll explore those techniques, as well as some of the major tools that implement them.

Text to Audio
Text-to-audio generation encompasses two overall modes: generating sound by descriptive prompt, or synthesizing text into speech. The former is similar to prompting an image-generation model: you describe what you want the audio output to be using words and terms that describe aspects of the desired sound. This can be everything from a sound effect (e.g., the sound of a door closing) to a musical snippet or even a full song. In the case of sound effects, your prompt might include the object that produces the sound effect (a door) and an action that is taken by or on the object (being closed). Music is more complex, and a prompt to generate music can include a wide array of artistic elements, such as:
• The genre (rock, pop, hip hop, etc.).
• The specific instruments (guitar, trombone, xylophone, etc.).
• The tempo (fast, mid, slow, etc.).
• The mood/tone (energetic, dark, relaxing, etc.).
• The era (60s, 70s, 80s, etc.).
• A place it's commonly associated with (gym, club, bar, etc.).
• The skill of the "musician" (beginner, intermediate, professional, etc.).
• Additional details (objects, actions, lyrics, etc.).
Just like with image generation, you can mix and match these prompt elements or omit certain ones as desired. Depending on the model, you can write these prompts as lists of words/terms or as complete sentences.
The other mode is text-to-speech synthesis. This does not involve describing the sound so much as providing the words that a simulated speaker should say, and in some cases, how they should say it. Depending on the model, you may be able to prompt a certain tone of voice or speaker mood.
Text-to-speech synthesis has been around for a long time, but it wasn't until recently that synthesized voices started becoming truly lifelike, able to mimic subtle variations in tone and emotion that are important in human speech.

Audio to Audio
There are a couple ways you could use audio as input for the purpose of getting audio as output. One is in a completion approach. This is similar to text completion, in that the model attempts to generate the next part of whatever it was prompted with. In the case of audio, you could send it a snippet of a song and then have the model continue that song for several seconds or minutes.
Another audio-to-audio approach would be to reconstruct the input in a different style or by including different details (e.g., different instruments). For example, you might generate a song that you like based on a text prompt, then send that song as input to recreate multiple variations of it.
Either of these audio-to-audio approaches can also be enhanced with the addition of a text prompt. For example, in audio completion, you could guide the model toward finishing a song in a certain way by specifying that the tempo should increase, or that the song should fade out, or that a new instrument should be introduced, and so on. Audio Editing and Cleaning
Like an image or a video, an audio file may need to be adjusted before it is an acceptable state. Non-AI programs can edit audio in a variety of ways, but AI can enhance some of the more difficult tasks. For example, removing audible background noise can be difficult, especially if there are other, desirable noises layered on top of it. A generative AI may be able to "clean" the audio by reconstructing the desirable sounds separate from the undesirable ones.
Another potential AI-driven task is to enhance overly compressed audio. When audio is heavily compressed, the dynamic range of the audio signal is reduced, leading to a loss of important information in the signal. This results in audio that sounds distorted and generally of poor quality. AI can address this issue in a way that is similar in concept to image and video upscaling or interpolation; it can attempt to add information to the signal and thereby increase its perceived quality.

Prompt Engineering for Audio Generation
Using prompts to generate audio is not as common as using prompts to generate other types of content, so there is not as much of a consensus on prompt engineering best practices as there is with text, code, and image generation. Nevertheless, there are still some tricks you should consider employing when generating audio from a prompt.
• Be descriptive, but experiment with variation: Just like other forms of generative AI, being descriptive when generating music and sound effects is crucial to getting the exact results you're looking for. However, you may not know exactly what you're looking for. For example, you may want music that fits a certain mood, but don't have any particular requirements beyond that. You could submit a prompt like "energetic rhythm" multiple times to get different results. The less detail you include, the more varied the output will be-which may be ideal in certain situations.
• Provide context clues for speech: When writing a script for text to speech, you may want the "speaker" to use a particular intonation. Depending on the model, you may be able to use tags to instruct the speaker to adopt emotions like sadness, anger, joy, etc. However, some models accept the spoken text only. If you want these models to produce speech with a certain emotion, you could try explicitly stating that in the text itself. For example, let's say you want the AI to speak the sentence, "Please take your seat." That sentence could be said in a warm and inviting way, or in a way that indicates the speaker is annoyed. To guide the AI to use the former intonation, you might rewrite the text to say, "I'm glad to see everyone today! Please take your seat." The first sentence is fairly unambiguous in expressing joy, so it provides context for the AI to use when speaking the second sentence.
• Use text to speech in chunks: Submitting multiple paragraphs to a text-to-speech AI is usually not a good idea. This will likely be less of an issue as technology advances, but for now, the quality of an AI-generated voice can degrade when it's given too much input. Instead, you might want to submit one or a few sentences at a time.
• Generate as many audio samples as you can: Like when generating images, the output from prompting an audio-generation model will probably not be perfect the first time. It may take several generations until an audio-generation model gives you really impressive results. Don't hesitate to prompt the model multiple times on the same or similar inputs.

Fine Tuning Audio Models
The overall process of fine tuning audio-generation models is fairly similar to tuning other types of generative AI. You collect data examples-in this case, audio files-that are relevant to the problem you're solving. For example, if you usually work with a specific genre of music, you might supply many audio snippets of music from that genre. The model's ability to generate music in this genre will be improved as a result.
Another goal of fine tuning audio models is to create more synthetic voices to use in a text-to-speech application. AI-driven speech synthesis tools usually have multiple voices the user can choose from. Each voice might approximate a certain gender, accent, pitch, etc. In order to generate more voices, you could train a model on data that includes different people speaking. You could also fine tune a model using voice cloning, which involves training a model on a specific person's voice so the model can mimic that person's voice when prompted. After training, cloned speech can be generated from a script, or, in some cases, from an audio voiceover.
In either case, the training data used for fine tuning a model should be of decent quality and labeled properly. In the case of a text-to-speech model, the label is typically just a transcription of what the speaker is saying, whereas with generating music and sound effects, the label is a description. In some scenarios, a large volume of audio data is required for successful fine tuning. However, some techniques (like voice cloning) require only a few seconds' worth of audio data to be successful.

Code Completion
Since code is itself a form of text, code-generation techniques are not too far off from text-generation techniques. One fairly simple application of code generation is code completion. Code completion has existed in a more primitive form for some time. Integrated development environments (IDEs), where programmers write their code, are often able to guess the next thing the programmer will type using context clues. Usually, however, this is limited to completing a single word or line, and the guesses are not always accurate. This is where generative AI can make improvements.

Prompt Engineering with Code Generation
Many of the prompt-engineering best practices that apply to text generation also apply to code generation: write clearly and precisely, know the model's limitations, and follow an iterative process. There are also some best practices that pertain to code generation specifically:
• Provide constraints and requirements: The output from a code-generation AI needs to be precise for it to be useful. Unlike natural language, code must be written in a certain way or it won't work. You can guide an AI toward this precision by being explicit about the constraints surrounding the coding problem and what your requirements are as far as the inputs, outputs, and conditions associated with that code.
• Format input code properly: You don't necessarily need to tell the model what programming language it should use to generate code if your prompt includes code as context. But, that will only happen if the code in your prompt follows the syntax and formatting conventions of your chosen programming language. Otherwise, the model may not output what you were expecting.
• Ask the AI to run the code: Some AI tools, particularly those that specialize in text generation, have the ability to run the code that they generate, output a result, send that result to the model for evaluation, and then retrieve a response from the model. If the AI doesn't run the code

TOPIC A
Identify Shortcomings of Generative AI
First, you'll identify the ways in which generative AI fails to perform to expectations. It's important that your perception of this new technology isn't based on exaggerated hype, but instead grounded in reality.

Confabulation/Hallucination
In generative AI, confabulation is the phenomenon in which a model produces an output that appears false, incoherent, or otherwise unjustifiable by the data it was trained on. It is based on the psychological term for a person whose memory issues cause them to unwittingly answer questions or provide explanations that are incorrect and/or do not make logical sense. In the media, confabulation is more commonly called hallucination, but this term is imprecise. When a person hallucinates, they experience or perceive an external stimulus that does not exist in reality; AI models are not capable of experience or perception in the way that humans are.
Confabulation can apply to many types of AI tasks, but it is most commonly associated with the outputs of large language models (LLMs). There are many different ways that confabulation can manifest in LLMs. Some examples include:
• Direct factual errors: For example, when asked to name the capital of New York State, the AI replies with "New York City." The capital is actually Albany.
• Indirect factual errors: For example, when asked to write an article on the history of New York State, the AI gets most of the details correct, but buried somewhere in the story it describes New York State as having a population of 15 million in 1900. In reality, the state's population was around 7 million in 1900.
• Continuing from false premises: For example, when asked to name all three capitals of New York State, the AI replies with "Albany, Buffalo, and Rochester." There is only one capital of New York State.
• Misinterpretation of user input: For example, when asked to name the second-largest city in New York State, the AI replies with "Rome." Technically, the city of Rome is the second-largest city in the state by area. But, the user probably meant "largest" in terms of population, which is Buffalo.


Note: These are just examples to illustrate possible confabulations; the model you use might answer these questions correctly.The exact reason for confabulation in AI is not fully understood, but researchers have suggested that models could confabulate due to flaws in the training process, like a failure in the encoder or decoder of a GPT architecture. Confabulation is one of the most significant issues affecting AI today, and it's something that every organization using generative AI must be vigilant for.

Misinformation and Misleading Content
Generative AI can be used to create and disseminate misinformation, fake news, and otherwise misleading content. Some chatbots have been configured to reject requests that violate ethical or propriety standards, but these restrictions are not perfect, and some users have found ways to circumvent them. A user could ask a chatbot, for example, to play a character and tell a story about some fictitious event that just happened. Then, the user publishes the story as a news article in order to trick readers into believing it really happened. Large groups of people, including corporations and governments, can use similar tactics to generate and spread propaganda.

Limitations of Training Data
There are many potential limitations of training data that can lead to undesirable outputs in a generative model. Outdated training data, as just mentioned, is one such problem. A model trained on sources up to some point in time will effectively have its knowledge base "frozen" at that point. Any events that happen after that date are unknown to the model. This could manifest as a problem in simple factual research, with the model unable to provide adequate answers to questions; or, the problem could be more subtle. The model might answer a question that would have been plausible at the time, but would now be considered outdated or even completely wrong.
The recency of the training data is not the only factor that can limit a model's effectiveness. The scope and diversity of that data is also a potential pitfall. One prominent example is that some image-generation models struggle to produce human hands. Hands often appear mangled, with extraneous fingers, missing fingers, fingers that are proportionally too long or too short, and so on. This is likely because most images of people-whether photographic or drawn-either do not feature a person's hands, or if they do, those hands are obscured in various ways. When you combine this with the fact that hands are fairly complex parts of the human body, you are left with models that give unconvincing results. Faces, on the other hand, are much more well represented in image datasets, so models have more success with them.

Other Quality Issues
There are other quality issues associated with generative AI models whose cause(s) are not easy to pin down. For example, chatbots like ChatGPT tend to respond to questions in a very rote way, with the same dry tone, and usually of a consistent length. This might be fine in plenty of situations, but it could also be fairly limiting in situations where creative and unique responses are sought after. Quality issues like these could be due to several factors, including the restrictions that organizations like OpenAI build into their products to prevent misuse.
There is also an element of randomness to generative outputs. You may have even experienced this as you've been prompting models. The same exact prompt fed to the same model at different times will likely lead to different outputs. In some cases, the differences are minor (e.g., wording changes in an LLM response). However, in other cases, the output may completely contradict prior outputs from the same prompt. For example, you ask the model what the largest planet in the solar system is. It correctly responds with "Jupiter." You ask the model the same question later, and it incorrectly responds with "Saturn." Issues like this may be the result of confabulation, but they are unique in the fact that they do not appear consistently. So, the model may have the appearance of authoritativeness, but in reality a "good" answer that it gives may have just been a fluke.
Industry-Specific Risks
When it comes to "bad" outputs-whether categorized as confabulations, misinformation, training-data limitations, or something else-certain industries are more at risk than others. Fields that prioritize creativity can actually harness these outputs to their advantage. For example, sometimes a generative model can output an image that it so unique that it has actual appeal, even if it isn't realistic. This isn't much different than humans creating art in avant-garde styles; Picasso's cubist paintings are unreal, yet considered great works of art.
On the opposite end, you have fields like law and medicine that deal with potentially severe consequences. For example, a Colombian judge caused controversy when he used ChatGPT to assist in a ruling involving the payment of medical expenses. If the AI were to confabulate in a case like this-or something even more serious-it could lead to real-world harm. It's important to consider how much error your particular organization and industry can tolerate when it comes to generative AI.

Vibe Coding
Vibe coding is a term coined in 2025 that can mean different things to different people. Generally, it refers to a human working with a code-generation model in a quick and collaborative, yet often careless, way. The main idea in vibe coding is that the human accepts the code written by the AI without truly understanding it. This typically happens with inexperienced programmers who haven't put in the time or effort to learn the complexities of programming, but it can also be the case for experienced programmers who are being lazy or are subject to significant time constraints.
Vibe coding has its benefits, namely that it can be truly helpful for new programmers to learn a language or to create and work with small snippets of low-impact code. But, vibe coding also has plenty of detractors, and for good reason. Using a code-generation tool for significant development
-especially code that will go to production and potentially have critical impacts-and not understanding that code, will likely have major negative consequences. This is due to issues that LLMs can have, like confabulation, use of outdated coding libraries and techniques, failure to account for new vulnerabilities, poor secure-coding practices (or none at all), and many more. A business application or module developed through vibe coding is likely to be flawed in many ways, and therefore will eventually cost the organization more time, money, and headache than if the code were written solely by skilled human programmers.

Cost of Implementation
There are several costs associated with implementing generative AI depending on how it is implemented. The most basic cost is signing up for a paid subscription to a cloud-based generative AI app. Such a cost might not be too prohibitive, especially in larger organizations. But, consider that some subscriptions apply per user, and if you need to make the app widely accessible to your workforce, that cost will increase significantly. Some services offer enterprise plans to meet this kind of need.
In many cases, an organization will not use an AI service's web app directly, but instead integrate its API into the organization's own app. This provides greater flexibility for the client organization, but it comes at a cost. The price per API call will likely be low, but a large volume of calls will quickly add up. An organization whose app is popular with customers, or is frequently used by internal staff, can quickly rack up hundreds or thousands of dollars in charges every day. For example, a popular customer-service chatbot that talks to many customers at the same time every business day will input and output a high number of tokens. Assuming the API cost is per token, the chatbot could potentially cost the organization more money than can be justified.
There is also cost associated with creating your own AI service infrastructure. Even if you license a model created by someone else (or rely on open-source models), you'll need to spend money on high-performance servers to host that model. Depending on how large and dispersed your audience is, you may need to build up an entirely new infrastructure to accommodate the bandwidth and processing needs. Likewise, you'll need to hire talent or assign existing talent to the development, maintenance, and support of this AI service infrastructure-all of which is expensive.

Hardware Requirements
If your organization relies entirely on cloud services for the processing of generative AI tasks, then your hardware requirements could be minimal. Any modern device that can run a web browser should be able to handle an AI service hosted by a third party and provided as a web app. If you rely on APIs to power in-house apps, your requirements start to increase. You'll need developer workstations, test servers, and deployment servers to facilitate your own app. However, these requirements apply to essentially any software development project. One requirement that stands out in a generative AI project is data storage. Most apps process and store data of some kind, but generative AI services can output an atypically large amount of data. For example, an app that generates batches of images to the user will need some place to store those images, at least temporarily. If these requests are high in volume, the organization may need a great deal of storage space to accommodate these outputs.
On the other hand, if you do any on-premises hosting of generative AI models, your hardware requirements will be considerable. Recall that general-purpose graphics processing units (GPUs) are at the heart of AI processing power. Without a powerful GPU (or other AI processor), a device may not even be able to obtain an output from a model. Now consider that hundreds or thousands of devices may be accessing your AI services. You'll need large clusters of GPUs to process these requests. Otherwise, you'd have to offload the GPU requirement to the client, which is just not feasible in many cases. And, you'll need enough bandwidth to service requests to clients over a network, given the amount of data that's involved in both input and output for generative models.
Requirements like these are why most organizations will rely on existing cloud services to facilitate generative AI requests.

Training Time and Output Time
Time is an important factor for any software project, but there are two particular ways that time can impact a generative AI project. The first involves training a model. Just like self-hosting a generative AI infrastructure, most organizations will choose not to train their own models. The data requirements are immense, and the hardware requirements just mentioned are as big of a concern for training a model as they are for putting the model into production. But, some organizations will train their own models-if not from scratch, then in a fine-tuning scenario. In either case, training millions or billions of parameters in a neural network takes a great deal of time.
For example, training a model as large as OpenAI's GPT-3 could take hundreds of years on a single top-of-the-line GPU. Only by running many GPU clusters in parallel is a more reasonable time achieved, but even so, it could have taken weeks or months. Fine tuning a foundational model will take less time than starting from scratch, but this can vary depending on how much data is used in the fine tuning.
The other aspect of generative AI where time becomes a challenge is waiting for model outputs. You may have experienced this as you've been using generative AI tools. The model might need some time to process a request before it can provide a result to this user. For example, depending on available hardware, it can take seconds, minutes, or even hours to denoise an image according to some text prompt. Delays due to high volumes of processing requests or spikes in bandwidth can also impact model output in a service architecture. It might take longer to come up with a good prompt and get a good result than if you had just looked for a free stock image that fulfills the same purpose.
An application that expects immediate output from a generative model could encounter problems, especially if that output needs to be sent to some other time-sensitive process. This is one of the reasons why major AI vendors offer their models in different sizes-smaller models take less time to process outputs. The tradeoff is that the smaller models may produce less useful results.

Lack of Fine-Tuned Control
Another shortcoming you might need to address is the lack of fine-tuned control in generative models. Although foundational models support fine tuning, not all generative AI models do. For whatever reason-time, cost, availability, etc.-your organization may have chosen to use a model that does not allow greater customization. In these cases, you'll need to rely on the general-purpose outputs of the model. Or, it may have already been fine tuned by a third party, and you have no control over the process. Either circumstance might be acceptable for some organizations, as fine tuning is not always necessary.
Even if you can fine tune a model, doing so is not always feasible. You need an adequate amount of data, and that data needs to be cleaned and properly formatted. The time and money spent on transforming data for fine tuning can be prohibitive for some organizations. So, you may need to settle for a foundational model or some third party's fine-tuned model. Alternatively, you may be able to rely on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) in text-based scenarios, assuming the model supports it.
Ultimately, even if you have access to a model that can be fine tuned and have the requisite data for fine tuning, the results are not guaranteed to be adequate. You may find that the customized model is no better at producing useful outputs in your specialized domain than the foundational model it was built on. In some cases, it may even perform worse. This happens when the model learns too much of the new training and isn't able to generalize to novel problems-an issue called overfitting.

There are some independent companies providing home-grown generative AI models and services, but they will likely not see the same widespread adoption. TOPIC B
Identify Ethical Risks of Generative AI
Aside from general shortcomings in performance and effectiveness, generative AI has plenty of ethical implications. Every organization that uses generative AI must identify these challenges in order to minimize the harm that generative AI can cause.

Ethics in AI
The ethics of AI involve thinking about how:
• Intelligent machines and humans interact with one another.
• Intelligent machines act as moral agents.
• AI may cause harm to humans, whether physical, psychological, financial, or other types of harm.
As a business decision maker, you won't necessarily be asked to take on the deep philosophical issues involved with AI. However, you are still responsible for ensuring that your AI solutions follow general ethical principles. In some organizations, ethics have actually become a standard part of corporate board reporting.
This course highlights five major ethical principles in emerging technologies like generative AI:
• Privacy
• Accountability
• Transparency and explainability
• Fairness and non-discrimination
• Safety and security
Artificial Intelligence Act
In August 2024, the AI Act came into force in the European Union, with additional provisions to follow in the coming years. The AI Act regulates providers of AI tools and services, with the exception of certain sectors like military and research. The regulations focus on a number of areas, but the core ethical principles they stem from include:
• Human agency and oversight
• Technical robustness and safety
• Privacy and data governance
• Transparency
• Diversity, non-discrimination, and fairness
• Societal and environmental well-being
• Accountability
For more information about Recital 27 of the EU AI Act, which discusses these non-binding principles, see https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/recital/27/.

Privacy Issues
Privacy focuses on protecting sensitive data about people from unauthorized or unwanted access. There are various ways in which generative AI can undermine privacy, including:
• Personal-data collection: Generative models require vast amounts of data, much of which may include sensitive information about people, including personally identifiable information (PII). This is especially true when data scientists indiscriminately scrape large swaths of data from the web to use to train a model. Many people might object to having their data be used without their consent.

• Personal-data leakage: There are essentially two ways that personal data used to train a model could leak to the public. The first involves a security breach targeting the organization that stores and maintains data used in training. For various reasons, the organization might keep this data around after a model has been trained. The other way personal data could be leaked is through the model itself. For example, if you ask a chatbot a question about a famous person, it might respond with the information you're looking for. It might be able to do the same for not-so-famous people whose data it happens to have been trained on. Of course, some datasets are publicly available from the outset, so you don't necessarily need an AI to find the personal information. But, an AI can make it easier to find.
• Personal-data inference. When you think of personal data, you might think of addresses, phone numbers, and so on. But a model doesn't need explicit examples of personal data to present a privacy risk. Remember, AI is capable of inference, which means it can analyze disparate information about individuals and piece that information together to form a logical conclusion. The conclusion might reveal something private.
• Personal-data confabulation: If a model confabulates when it comes to an individual's personal data, it might provide incorrect results. Even if the model is incorrectly identifying a person, it could still point to another person whose private data is being exposed. For example, it might provide a name, address, and phone number. If two of three are correct, an attacker could still use that for unethical purposes, and perhaps even discover the third factor.
• Content generation using personal data: Even if the model and the training data are clear of privacy violations, a malicious user could still prompt the model with someone else's personal data, then generate content with that personal data for abusive purposes. For example, the malicious user might clone a specific person's voice, then use that voice without their permission.
There are ways to limit privacy issues in generative models. For example, most prominent AI organizations anonymize the training data and strip out potentially private details in training. They could also place similar limitations on the operation of the model itself, such as triggering the model to refuse requests that involve private data. However, this is less effective since there are ways of circumventing such limitations.

Accountability Issues
Accountability is all about ensuring people are held responsible for their actions. AI can make accountability a challenge, since decisions of great consequence are made by machines and not people. For example:
• Attribution of generated content: One issue that is frequently discussed when it comes to generative AI, particularly AI art, is who gets the credit (or the blame) for generated content? Not just in a legal sense, but from an ethical standpoint. If an organization publishes AI-generated content that inflicts or inspires real-world harm, who is held accountable? It could be argued that no one really "created" the work in the same way that a human would, so the same ethical standards may not apply. And, one could argue that those who trained the model on potentially faulty data should bear some of the responsibility.
• Misuse of generated content: A piece of generated content might be perfectly fine when it's placed in the proper context. However, it could be misappropriated to be deceptive or spread false information when placed in other contexts. Determining and/or proving the intent behind the original content and the altered content is not easy to do, especially since there is no human "author" in the traditional sense.
• Unforeseen consequences: Even when generated content is not misused, at least not intentionally, it could still cause problems. For example, a generated image may violate cultural norms in one region but be perfectly acceptable in another. Or, generating large volumes of text could include profanity and other offensive language. Tracing these issues back to one person is not always reasonable.
• User-driven content generation: You might reasonably expect that an organization will take collective responsibility for issues with generative AI if they are the ones who generated and published the content. But, what if you develop an app that enables users to generate content

Accountability issues are usually addressed by establishing a clear chain of responsibility within the organization. The issue of authorship may not even be relevant; the focus may instead shift toward who published or authorized the publishing of the content.

Transparency and Explainability Issues
Transparency is about enabling people to see into the inner workings of the technology and its effects on people and society. Explainability is about being able to truly understand and interpret how the technology works and why. The complex nature of AI can make supporting these principles a challenge.
• Black-box systems: A common problem in deep learning and neural networks is the black-box problem, which refers to the difficulty (or impossibility) for a human practitioner to determine why the model made a certain decision. Recall that generative models are neural networks made up of potentially billions of parameters, and that all of these parameters influence the results that the model outputs. Thoroughly understanding this level of complexity is just not feasible. So, it may be unreasonable to "trust" the model. If the output from the model is bad (e.g., a confabulation), then fixing the problem may not be plausible.
• Closed-source systems: Sometimes, limitations are imposed on the organizations that create generative AI systems. They may refuse to publicly disclose details like the source of the training data, the size of the training data, the size of the model, the efforts taken to address potential ethical issues, and so on. This lack of transparency can also make it difficult for users to trust the output of a model that they know little about.
• Performance tradeoffs: Another way that AI organizations undermine transparency and explainability is by trading off these principles for better model performance. Using techniques to simplify a model usually results in it being more easily explainable. But, it can also reduce the effectiveness of its outputs. In a highly competitive marketplace like AI, the organization may choose to prioritize performance over anything else.
• Big data: Big data is crucial for training high-performance generative models, but being able to peer inside that data and understand it at certain levels of magnification is very difficult. The sheer scope of the data involved prevents any one person from having a comprehensive understanding of its contents.
Some software tools can enable better transparency and explainability in AI models, but these do not perfectly solve the problem. There is also the explainable AI (XAI) initiative, which seeks to develop and employ tools and techniques that limit the black-box problem and promote transparency in AI systems.

Fairness and Non-Discrimination Issues
The principles of fairness and non-discrimination ensure people are not treated improperly because of who they are. They are primarily undermined by bias, which is a major issue in AI. For example:
• Training-data bias: Generative models, like all AI models, learn from data that is provided to them. The data is typically fed from a human, or is at least the product of some prior human guidance. This means the data has the potential to exhibit bias, or undue positive or negative attitudes applied to individuals, groups, or ideas. So, if the model is trained on biased data, it will produce biased results.
• Representation disparities: If the training data is biased in such a way that it prioritizes or deprioritizes a certain group, that group will not be properly represented in the model's outputs. For example, an image-generation model may have been trained on pictures of people. Most of those pictures depict people of a specific ethnicity. Prompting the model to generate "a person," without any ethnicity qualifiers, may lead the model to outputting people of that single ethnicity, excluding all others.

• Stereotype propagation: Even if the training data represents diverse groups of people, the character of that representation might itself exhibit bias. For example, a text-generation model is trained on social media posts. Those posts might express negative stereotypes of people of certain nationalities. If the model is prompted to describe a nation's culture, it might use those negative stereotypes to describe that culture.
• RLHF bias: Systems that rely on RLHF to "improve" a model may be subject to a phenomenon by which humans subconsciously embed their own values into their feedback. If those values include certain biases, the model may be reinforced to learn those biases and therefore generate outputs that propagate exclusionary and discriminatory attitudes.
• Lack of fairness metrics: Acknowledging that a model or the data it was trained on has bias issues is one thing. Actually evaluating the model for these issues is another. Without well-defined metrics for identifying and quantifying bias in data or a model, it is difficult to address the problem.
Reducing bias in generative models usually involves taking more active steps to identify bias in training data, before the model is built on that data. Once the model is built, the organization can also run tests on its outputs to detect any lingering bias.

Safety and Security Issues
Security is all about protecting the organization's assets. Safety is related in that it is about protecting the human asset. In other words, minimizing the chance that people are physically harmed and that property (whether physical or virtual) is compromised. Some ways that generative AI can challenge safety and security include:
• Malicious content generation: Malicious users might be able to use an organization's generative AI systems to create content that violates security and safety principles. For example, an operating system might use facial recognition to authenticate a user. An attacker could generate multiple images or even video of a person's face, from different angles and poses, to fool the authentication system.
• Unintended consequences: Consider that the output of a generative AI, particularly code, will be used to automate processes that are potentially hazardous. For example, the operation of manufacturing equipment might use AI-generated code. If this code were to be buggy or not properly designed, it could cause serious injury to someone operating the machine it runs on.
• Cloud security risks: Organizations that rely on cloud services for generative AI must accept the security risks that come along with them. The client organization will likely not have control over what security mechanisms are put in place by the cloud provider, nor will they have the ability to audit the provider's security. In other words, the client is at the mercy of the provider. Poor security on the provider's part will have a downstream effect on its clients.
• Adversarial attacks: As mentioned earlier, attackers can exploit generative AI services using techniques like prompt injection. This can lead to sensitive-data leakage, damage to data or systems, and more. Attackers can also poison models by compromising the data used in training, or during the RLHF process.
There are various security controls and techniques that an organization can put in place to mitigate these issues, assuming the organization is in full control of generative AI operations. For example, to mitigate the effects of prompt injection, an app might be able to detect malicious injection attempts (perhaps using AI), or it might warn the user and require human confirmation before actually performing a consequential task. These techniques are not guaranteed to work in all circumstances, however.

Identify Business Concerns of Generative AI
Identifying general shortcomings and ethical issues surrounding generative AI is not enough to avoid trouble. In this topic, you'll learn about the different ways in which generative AI can be challenging to implement from a business perspective.

Governance
Governance is all about putting various mechanisms, processes, and structures in place to direct and control the activities of an organization. There are many potential aspects of governance, such as defining roles and responsibilities, addressing stakeholder interests, upholding organizational integrity and ethical principles, and so on.
A disruptive technology like generative AI poses significant challenges to governance. Many of the aforementioned issues are involved in this. In other words, ethical issues like bias and misuse, as well as performance issues like confabulation and misleading content, must be addressed through an organization's governance frameworks. This ensures the issues are addressed from the top down, consistently, and with the resources necessary to do the job. Otherwise, the organization's efforts to implement generative AI will be poorly managed and disconnected from the overall goals of the business.
However, this is easier said than done. There are many unique challenges in incorporating generative AI into a governance framework. Some of the major challenges include:
• Impact on employees
• Future of work
• Operational risks

Impact on Employees
One of the most pressing issues of introducing generative AI, especially agentic AI, in the business is its effect on human labor. This mostly includes white-collar jobs, but may also affect blue-collar jobs. The jobs most at risk are those that produce some amount of intellectual content, especially if the production of that content involves a lot of repetitive or time-consuming behavior.
Naturally, your employees may feel anxiety (sometimes called technostress) as some of their job functions become more and more automated. Any one of these symptoms can negatively affect employees' productivity-for example, they may be more likely to commit errors or lose focus on the task at hand. They may also seek employment elsewhere if they feel their health is suffering.
Even if employees don't fear being replaced by AI, they may still struggle to adapt to it. They likely are used to following a specific set of processes that require their full input and perhaps some degree of creativity. Generative and agentic AI will have a profound impact on the future of work-how it's conducted and who is conducting it. Data Risks
Because generative AI is so data driven, the data itself becomes a crucial asset worthy of your special attention. This includes both data used in training and fine tuning, as well as the outputs from a generative model (text, code, images, etc.). It's likely that your organization, through its IT team, has some existing processes in place to protect data. However, the requirements and parameters of those data-protection processes may change as a result of integrating generative AI into the business.
Some areas of concern include:
• Data quality: The data must meet certain criteria, such as: accuracy, recentness, completeness, formatting, volume, and usability. It's up to you and your team to determine which of these criteria are at greatest risk, as well as how to address that risk. For example, volume might not be as much of a concern if you're not training a model. Or, the accuracy of text output from a model might be of primary concern in a research scenario.
• Data control: Data in the organization's possession must be tightly controlled, particularly if that data is of a sensitive or private nature. Some common data-control techniques include: acquisition, storage, ownership, permissions, and disposal. For example, who has permission to access the outputs of a generative model? • Data security: The more data you work with, the more you put at risk. Data is at risk of being: copied by and disseminated to unauthorized users, modified in unauthorized ways, deleted without authorization, and made unavailable for use. For example, an attacker could breach a database that holds data used in training, then tamper with the data in order to influence what the model learns.

Brand Reputation and Consumer Trust
The use of generative AI can lead to risks that come from external sources, particularly customers and the public at large. People may be leery of generative AI, seeing it as a poor replacement for human creativity or as an intrusive force that devalues human work. Any organization that implements generative AI will need to be prepared to receive critical feedback or outright hostility from consumers.
The use of generative AI can negatively impact brand reputation in various ways. For example, a company uses generated imagery in an advertisement. The imagery isn't bad quality per se, but something about it looks off to most observers. They might be able to tell that the imagery was created by an AI. The advertisement (and by extension, the company) might appear cheap and amateurish, leading to a negative reaction that impacts sales of the advertised product/service.
Even when generated content is fairly realistic, people may still react negatively if they believe it is generated by an AI. For example, a company starts implementing a chatbot as the first line of response for customer inquiries. As helpful as the chatbot might be, the customer thinks that the company is cutting corners, putting people out of jobs, or just generally being greedy. Dissatisfaction can spread fairly quickly in the age of social media, causing consumer trust in the organization to fall.

They want organizations to guarantee that generated content will not be misused in ways that impact them directly or impact groups they are affiliated with. Intellectual Property and Copyright
The status of intellectual property (IP) and copyright in the advent of AI-generated content is still hotly contested. At the time of writing, there are several lawsuits pending that challenge notions of IP ownership and copyright infringement with regard to the data used to train AI and the output that is generated by AI.
For example, in January 2023, three artists filed a lawsuit against Stability AI and Midjourney for copyright infringement. They claimed that these companies violated the copyrights of millions of artists whose images were scraped from various image-hosting websites without the copyright holders' permission-images that were used to train their image-generation models. According to the litigants, these tools merely create derivative works of the images that they were trained on. If true, this would mean that the original copyright holders would own any works generated using those tools, and not the company that owns the models, nor the user(s) that generated the images.
Critics of lawsuits like these maintain that they are based on a fundamental misunderstanding of the technology. Model checkpoints, for example, only store parameters learned during training-they do not store images, or any other type of content. They also argue that the content generated from models is suitably transformative and would qualify for fair-use protection, even if it mimics certain styles or has recognizable compositions.
This likewise raises another point: issues of copyright in the training data aside, who owns the created work? The organization that owns and operates the model, or the user who generated the work? What constitutes the "creation" of a work, for that matter? It could be argued that the work involved in training a model to produce some desired output qualifies. Or, that the user who thought of and then submitted the prompt is the one doing the creative work. Others might argue that no one can claim ownership of the IP; that the nature of AI generation means there is no human effort that manifests the created content.
These issues won't be solved overnight, but it is important for organizations to at least consider them and how they could impact the business.
Depiction of Copyrighted Material
An unrelated copyright issue involves the depiction of logos and personal likenesses that are copyrighted. For example, someone might prompt an image-generation model to create a picture of a laptop. Due to the abundance of training images depicting MacBook(r) devices, the model may strongly associate laptops with the Apple(r) logo, and thus paint that logo into the image, even when not directly prompted to do so. Usage of such an image for commercial purposes may violate copyright.

Legal and Regulatory Considerations
Aside from copyright laws, there are other legal ramifications to consider when implementing generative AI in the organization. Traditional regulations and other legal frameworks are not necessarily suitable to cover the unique aspects of generative AI, so new ones need to be created. The problem is, technology always seems to outpace the law, and generative AI is no exception. Yet, although laws regulating generative AI and AI generally were scarce a few years ago, this is no longer the case. The EU AI Act is fully in force, and U.S. states like Colorado, California, Utah, and Tennessee have enacted binding AI legislation. Internationally, treaties like the Council of Europe's Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law also impose enforceable rules. There are undoubtedly more regulations to come.

Implement Change Management
Change management is a structured approach to facilitating changes to critical aspects of an organization, including its operations, personnel, data, processes, procedures, policies, and more. It ensures the smooth transition of any one or all of these aspects, minimizing the amount of disruption to the business that transition can cause. Because generative AI, and agentic AI in particular, will have an impact on the way an organization functions, it must be carefully integrated into a change-management process.
There are a few ways that stakeholders like yourself can promote change management in generative AI projects. For the most part, you should ensure that all affected personnel have one or more key roles to play in the process. For example:
• Change agents: A change agent is anyone, internal or external, who advocates, champions, or impels change within the organization. They are typically responsible for facilitating communication, whether between employees or between the organization and outside stakeholders; providing and soliciting feedback; and generally encouraging discussions about change. For generative AI, change agents can help ensure that the organization has all the resources it needs to explore its options, and that all personnel are educated on generative AI according to their role.
• Knowledge translators: A knowledge translator is someone who can distill knowledge into a form that is more easily put into practice. The target audience of a translator can be technical practitioners, managers, end users-anyone who needs to know some bit of information to be effective in their role, but cannot be expected to synthesize that information on their own. In terms of generative AI, a knowledge translator could be someone with technical expertise who is able to translate new advancements in generative AI to key decision makers within the organization. Or, it could be someone like a manager, who needs to disseminate basic knowledge of generative AI to their team members in anticipation of a new generative AI system that will affect those team members' jobs.
• Humans in the loop: The idea of "humans in the loop" refers to the desire for there to be human oversight at all significant stages of an automated process. The purpose is to protect people and organizations from harms that could arise by allowing automated processes to replace human judgment rather than enhance it. Anyone can be a candidate for being kept in the loop, depending on their role, the use case for the generative solution, and how the solution is implemented. Change-management processes should ensure that humans are kept in the loop for generative AI, and agentic AI even more so due to its high level of automation. This can help mitigate issues like confabulation, training bias, operational costs, privacy risks, and so on.

TOPIC B
Evaluate the Results of Generative AI Projects
You may have successfully implemented generative AI into your business operations, but you're not done. You still need to ensure the continued success of those operations. So, in this topic, you'll evaluate the performance and results of your generative AI projects.

Assess Business Impact
Assessing business impact helps the organization determine whether or not the generative AI solution is accomplishing the goals set out for it, and to what degree it is (or is not) providing value to the business. It also helps identify business functions that are susceptible to disruption and loss. A business-impact analysis (BIA) traditionally focuses on that last point, but can also draw general conclusions about how the business is affected by major changes from both internal and external sources.
Once a generative AI solution is put into place, it can be analyzed periodically for its impact on the business. Key aspects of this analysis might include:
• The degree to which project goals and objectives have been achieved. This can be a "pass/fail" system or scored using numbers that indicate relative success or failure. For example, if a goal was to increase customer retention, the impact analysis might indicate a percentage increase or decrease in customer retention after the solution was put into place.
• Tracing goal outcomes to supporting objectives. If a goal fails or is not adequately met, then this usually points to a problem with specific objectives. After all, objectives are put in place to accomplish goals. If a solution failed to increase customer retention, then it could be due to the failure of an objective, like "generate images that are relevant to the customer's product interests."
• Identifying the return on investment (ROI). Time, effort, and money are all necessary to get any project off the ground, generative AI being no exception. By quantifying the financial impact of the solution, as well as the revenue that the solution helped to generate, the organization is in a better position to assess its impact.
• Identifying ethical and brand-related impacts. As you know, generative AI has plenty of risks attached to it. If those risks became a reality, the organization should find out in its analysis. It also needs to identify the damage those risks caused, whether it's damage to brand reputation or direct financial damage.
• Ultimately, whether or not key decision makers believe that the solution has had an overall positive effect on the business. This is a subjective assessment and can vary between decision makers, but it is worth considering.
Assessing these aspects will help the organization make more informed decisions regarding its generative AI solutions. The conclusions of the analysis might prompt the organization to take any number of actions: keep the solution at status quo, expand its capabilities, limit its reach, scrap it entirely, etc.

Perform Comparative Analysis
A comparative analysis involves evaluating the performance of a generative AI solution against alternative solutions. It essentially helps the organization determine whether or not generative AI is worth investing time, effort, and money on to solve business problems and accomplish goals. Or, if non-generative AI solutions might be able to fulfill those same business needs more quickly, and at reduced cost.

The exact nature of the comparative analysis will differ widely based on the problem(s) the business is trying to solve, the generative solution(s) it's tried, and the available alternative(s). However, there are some general best practices that most comparative analyses can make use of, including:
• Create a baseline of acceptable results from which you evaluate the generative solution and its alternative(s). Whether solutions are above or below the baseline determines their usefulness to the business.
• Perform A/B testing. An A/B test presents different content to different people to determine which content leads to the best results. For example, on a website, you might show one segment of customers only human-created photos of your products, whereas another segment is only shown AI-generated images of the products. If the segment that saw human-created photos has much higher retention, it might indicate that a non-generative solution is a better option.
• Track efficiency gains and losses when using a solution. For example, an image generator might take much less time to create useful images than a human artist, but writing an effective prompt to get those images takes the user a long time. The efficiency gains in one area may or may not compensate for the efficiency losses in another area.
In addition to comparing a generative solution to a non-generative one, you could also analyze the performance of two competing generative solutions. For example, a chatbot using one company's model might prove to be less effective at engaging with customers than another company's model. Or, perhaps you are comparing two different generative AI modalities to see which one is providing the most value.

Collect User Feedback
User feedback is a valuable tool for assessing any project. For generative AI, the organization can assess users' feelings toward the solution, and whether or not they believe the solution is enhancing their productivity or otherwise making their experience a better one. Both internal and external personnel, as well as customers, will potentially use your AI solution, so you should begin by identifying who your users are.
Once you've identified who your users are, there are various ways of collecting user feedback, including:
• Surveys and questionnaires: You can design and distribute surveys and questionnaires that ask users who interact with the AI solution various questions, including how satisfied they are using the solution, how easy or difficult it is to use the solution, whether or not they think the solution is better than previous ones, and any other feedback they might wish to share. There are various survey tools that can help you design and distribute surveys through the web, through email, or through an app.
• Interviews and focus groups: In some cases, you may have the opportunity to acquire more direct and personal feedback from your users. You could set up one-on-one interviews. Or, you could set up focus groups of a few people to get multiple points of view at once. In either case, you will be able to probe deeper into their feedback and lead a discussion, which can provide you with greater insight than surveys.
• Online reviews and feedback: Other than reaching out to users directly, you may want to monitor various social media platforms and discussion sites to see what users are saying about the solution, assuming that solution is publicly accessible. Their comments, when taken in the aggregate, can indicate an overall level of satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the solution, as well as reveal common issues or strengths.
• Customer support channels: Depending on the nature of the generative AI solution, it may be included in helpdesk or other customer-service-oriented support channels. You might also be able to interview the support personnel directly to get their impressions on what customers need.

Monitor and Analyze Usage of Generative AI Systems
Once you've deployed a generative AI solution to a user base, whether part of a beta test or in a final release, you should collect data on how people are using the solution. This will enable you to capture useful information about the solution that direct user feedback can't necessary provide. You can use this information to identify important factors like performance, areas for improvement, and user behavior-all of which can guide decisions concerning future versions of the solution.
The following are some tactics for monitoring and analyzing usage of generative AI systems:
• Track user and system behavior: If possible, you should integrate mechanisms for tracking usage within the AI solution itself, or as a system that can hook into the solution. This might take the form of logs that record specific actions and events, like when the user marks a generative output as being "good" or "bad" in an RLHF environment, or when an agent updates its tasks based on contextual changes in an agentic environment. Whatever form the tracking mechanism takes, it should be able to track user and system behavior without negatively impacting the experience.
• Monitor system performance: Besides tracking direct user actions, you should also collect data on the performance of an AI solution as it is being used. This might include the delay between input and output, any network connection issues with a backend cloud service, and so on.
• Continuously monitor usage: Many AI solutions receive updates and other changes over time that can have an impact on usage. As such, it's important to continuously monitor usage over time so that the organization can assess whether or not these changes are having a positive effect.
• Process and store usage data: As you collect usage data, you'll need some place to store it. And, you may need to process that data so it is easier to analyze. You might set up data pipelines that can ingest usage data, transform it as necessary, then send it to a database-all in an automated fashion. However you store and process the data, it should be secured and managed appropriately, particularly if that data has privacy implications.
• Perform data analysis: Once you've collected and processed usage data, you can begin analyzing it. You can use statistical techniques to identify which features are used most frequently and least frequently, for example. Or, how long a session lasts on average. You can also analyze the data for overall usage patterns, like how users typically navigate through the solution.
• Define relevant metrics: As you collect and analyze data, you'll need to determine which metrics are most relevant to the usage of your AI solution. That way, you're not measuring usage behaviors that aren't helpful or that don't pertain to your specific solution.

Measure Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)
Key performance indicators (KPIs) are metrics that align with business goals and objectives. They are a crucial component to evaluating not just the relative performance of a solution, but also the way in which that solution is or is not bringing value to the business. When it comes to generative AI, there are several KPIs you might consider. Which ones you use will depend on the modality, architecture, and business goals/objectives of the solution:
• User-engagement metrics: The number of interactions with the solution (e.g., number of prompts); the average time spent in a session (e.g., conversing with a chatbot); the number of different features used in an app; etc.
• Quality metrics: Objective measurements of quality (e.g., the technical accuracy of text output); subjective measurements of quality (e.g., whether an image appears realistic or not); etc.
• Efficiency metrics: The average processing time spent generating an output (e.g., CPU and GPU cycles); the amount of memory used in a period of time; the network bandwidth consumed in a period of time; etc.

Identify Project Limitations
There are several potential limitations to generative AI projects that you should be aware of. You can identify these limitations using many of the aforementioned techniques: assess business impacts, perform comparative analyses, collect user feedback, monitor and analyze usage, and so on. Some other methods for identifying limitations include:
• Perform extensive testing of all generative AI solutions, as well as any systems that support those solutions. These tests should focus on ensuring the solution is free of software bugs, but also on the content and quality of generative outputs. For example, in a text-generation solution, you should submit diverse sets of prompts, including prompts that may seem unusual, to determine if the model is unable to cope with edge cases.
• Consult with AI experts who can evaluate the solution from a technical standpoint. They should be able to identify weaknesses inherent in the technology, as well as any weaknesses in the way the organization is using the technology.
• Consult with business stakeholders regarding the ability for the organization to effectively implement and maintain a generative AI solution. It's possible that the organization simply doesn't have the funds, resources, or time to build or incorporate the ideal solution. So, you may need to work around these business limitations by accepting limitations in the solution itself.
There are many potential limitations to a generative AI project, several of which have already been discussed. Recall issues such as confabulation, poor-quality outputs, delays in training and output, lack of fine-tuned control, ethical violations, operational risks, etc. If any of these issues affect the generative AI solution, they must be identified as early in the process as possible.

Identify Undesirable Outcomes
In the event that a generative AI solution leads to undesirable outcomes, you need to recognize those outcomes and their impact on the business. Many of these outcomes have been discussed in some capacity: brand damage, user mistrust, financial loss, workforce disruption, regulatory violations, copyright infringement, and more.
The following are some tactics for identifying undesirable outcomes stemming from generative AI:
• Clearly define criteria for what constitutes an "undesirable outcome" in the context of generative AI.
• Monitor public perceptions of the solution for common pain points using social media and other sources of online discourse.
• Solicit feedback from a variety of stakeholders to detect undesirable outcomes that are otherwise likely to go unnoticed.
• Conduct regular audits and reviews of outputs from generative AI to ensure they are meeting quality standards.
• Watch for signs of drift in an agentic system, such as agents optimizing unintended goals or automating workflows that should be performed by humans.

Assess Long-Term Sustainability
Your generative AI solution may have proven effective in the short term, but unless you can prove it has long-term viability, it may not be considered a success. Unless you plan on using a generative AI solution temporarily or just as a test run, you'll probably want to incorporate it into wider business operations so its results can bring continued and repeated value to the business.
To assess the long-term sustainability of a generative AI solution, you might ask key questions such as:
• Can the organization estimate with high probability that the solution will continue to be cost effective over months or years?
• Are there enough resources and personnel to maintain the solution over a long period of time?
• Is there a long-term plan provided by the AI vendor, or by the organization itself, for continual updates to the solution?
• Will there be a steady enough flow of training data over time to meet the solution's needs, if applicable?
• Can the solution consistently meet user needs and engender customer satisfaction?
• Is the solution designed in such a way that it can be forward compatible with new systems and frameworks?
• Can the solution sustain the organization's commitment to ethics and security over a long period of time?
• Can the solution be easily adapted to changing business needs?

ACTIVITY 6-1: Identifying Generative AI Shortcomings

1. What could be causing these issues, and how might they impact the business?
A: Answers may vary, but incorrect or misleading information generated from an AI could be due to flaws in the data it was trained on. If that data includes misinformation, the model might learn that misinformation as if it were true. Alternatively, the model might be experiencing confabulation-bad outputs that are not due to poor training data.
Confabulations are difficult to anticipate and eliminate. Whatever the cause may be, the organization is risking its reputation by publishing false information. Users may distrust the organization's posts, making that marketing initiative fruitless. Or, worse yet, that distrust could affect the entire organization itself, leading to a decline in sales as customers take their business elsewhere.
2. What are some potential pitfalls of operating your own generative AI infrastructure?
A: Answers may vary, but the cost of implementation is one major concern. If the organization is going to operate the models themselves, they will need to spend money on high-performance servers and suitable network bandwidth to host and deliver the models to users. They will also need to spend money on qualified personnel who can build and maintain the infrastructure. The hardware requirements for the servers are also fairly steep compared to other business operations.
3. What might be causing these delays, and what impact might they have on the customer experience?
A: Answers may vary, but output delays can be due to a number of factors. The backend model might be receiving too many requests for it to process at one time, or there could be issues with the cloud provider's network infrastructure. This is a risk of relying on a cloud provider; you don't have total control over the performance of their systems and networks. In an online ordering app, a significant delay in the chatbot's responses could frustrate customers, leading them to cancel the transaction. If this happens to enough customers, it could result in a major loss of sales revenue.

ACTIVITY 7-1: Applying Best Practices for Generative AI in the Organization

1. How can you help the sales team select the right generative AI tool for their needs?
A: Answers may vary, but determining the relevant modality or modalities is a good place to start. In this case, the sales team probably needs either a text-generation AI or a code-generation AI. A text-generation AI might produce conclusions from the data in a natural language, whereas a code-generation AI could provide the functions or formulas they need to use in the spreadsheet to get what they're looking for. You also need to think about how the tool will ingest the input. If there's a lot of data stored in spreadsheets, it might not be feasible to copy and paste that data into a chatbot web app. Instead, an AI that integrates directly with the spreadsheet app may be ideal, assuming such a thing is available for the spreadsheet software they're using. Of course, they also need to consider the tool's pricing structure. Is it a flat subscription fee, or will they be charged per token or some other measurement of input/output?
2. How might you ensure that all employees in the organization are properly educated on the basics and use of generative AI?
A: Answers may vary, but this kind of initiative needs to come from the top down as part of governance practices. The organization's leadership must start fostering interest in generative AI, as well as explain how generative AI can help employees and subsequently make the business more competitive. As far as actual tactics to educate employees, the organization (or a contractor) could deliver training programs that go over the fundamentals of generative AI and how to use it effectively. The organization can also provide resources to employees who want to learn more, such as documentation. And, the organization could also encourage employees to share what they've learned with each other by holding workshops or webinars.
3. How might you assess the design of this prototype to ensure any issues will be addressed in the final release?

In either case, how does each environment scale to meet demand? Will an on-premises infrastructure be too challenging to scale up? Will a cloud environment be too costly to scale up? Another concern is how the two models are integrated. The LLM needs to output a description in a way that is suitable for the image generator to produce good results. Does the app take that into account? Also, what if one model is updated-will it break the functionality of the other model? Maintenance is another design factor that you need to assess. Are there enough qualified internal personnel to support the app over its lifespan? What will it cost to maintain the app? Ultimately, the user experience itself also needs to be assessed. It should be clear how the app is meant to provide value to the target user, not just be a novelty that users will get bored with after a while.

ACTIVITY 7-2: Evaluating the Results of Generative AI Projects

1. What are some KPIs that could help you evaluate the success of this generative AI project?
A: Answers may vary, but there are various KPIs that the organization could collect. For example, the output from the model should be accurate and not introduce any false, misleading, or incomplete content. If developers make mistakes and spend time trying to fix them, you could track those mistakes back to the reference materials that the model produced. The frequency of these mistakes could indicate how accurate or inaccurate the reference materials are. Another objective is to ensure that the reference materials are not only accurate, but actually saving the developers time as well. You could track how much time developers spend on their tasks, then compare the before and after. If the developers complete their tasks more quickly on average with the new references materials, you could call the project a success.
2. How might you evaluate usage of the app and collect user feedback?
A: Answers may vary, but to collect user feedback, you could take the direct route and invite random users to fill out surveys. The surveys would ask questions about each user's experience and any issues or functionality they would like addressed. You can also track user reviews on the associated app store(s) or social media posts in which the app is mentioned. To evaluate usage of the app, you could monitor and analyze aspects such as how long users typically spend in the app, how many times a user typically edits an image before they are satisfied, how often users experience excess delays in their requests, what type of images are most commonly edited, what type of edits are most commonly requested, and so on. These will help you obtain a better picture of how people are using your app as well as any deficiencies in the user experience.
3. How might you assess the long-term sustainability of this chatbot?
A: Answers may vary, but there are a few things that could affect the chatbot's sustainability over the long term. For example, the chatbot might be cost effective now, but you need to consider that the benefits it provides won't always exceed the cost of operating it. This is especially true if the organization decides to constantly maintain and update the chatbot to keep it at peak performance. That raises another concern, too. Is it worth the expense to constantly fine tune and update the chatbot on new data so that it doesn't appear obsolete or give poor-quality answers? Is it even feasible for the organization to do this themselves? Also, you need to think about the possibility that business needs will change. Perhaps the chatbot will be expected to take on more and more queries, or more complex queries that would have been routed to a human agent in the past. Will the chatbot be able to handle these new circumstances? Ultimately, the long-term sustainability of a chatbot, or any generative AI solution, depends on how much value it can continue to provide to the business.

In deep learning, a neural network that uses
self-attention to process input data.

Udio
A music-generation service developed by the
company of the same name and released in 2024.
unsupervised learning
A type of machine learning in which label
values are not provided as input, so the model does not have an explicit variable that it is predicting.
VAE
(variational autoencoder) A neural-network
architecture in which two networks, the encoder and the decoder, work together to generate images and other output.
Veo
A video-generation model developed by
Google DeepMind.
vibe coding
The practice of working with an AI code-
generation tool in a quick and careless way, without truly understanding the code it produces.
voice cloning
The technique of training a model on audio
clips of a person's voice so that the model can accurately mimic that person's voice when prompted.
weight
In an artificial neural network, a type of model
parameter that indicates the strength of the connection between neurons.
Whisper
A family of open-source, speech-to-text
models developed by OpenAI and released in 2022.
XAI
(explainable artificial intelligence) An initiative
that seeks to develop and deploy tools and techniques for countering the black-box problem and promoting transparency in AI systems.
zero-shot prompting
A prompting technique in which no examples
are used to guide the model's outputs.
